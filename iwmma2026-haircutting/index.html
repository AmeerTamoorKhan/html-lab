A robot that trims, the hair grows back, and it trims again

This talk is about a simple-sounding question: can a robot give you a haircut?

Cutting hair is something people do every day, but for a robot it is one of the hardest jobs there is. The "material" is thousands of soft, moving strands on a living, moving head, and mistakes near ears and eyes are not acceptable.

We'll walk from the classical engineering view (treat it like precision machining) to the newest AI view (a robot that understands "short on the sides, keep the top" and acts on it).

Thesis: robotic haircutting is a closed-loop, safety-critical manipulation problem on a deformable, non-stationary workpiece. It can be decomposed into localisation, hair modelling, coverage/toolpath planning and compliant control (CNC and mobile-robotics lenses), and then lifted into a learned policy, with teleoperation data as the bridge to vision-language-action (VLA) models.

The animation is a toy version of that loop: a 2-link arm follows a coverage path while strands regrow, so the system has to cut repeatedly until it converges to a target length.

Why · the challenge

Hair is the worst workpiece imaginable

Compare a part on a CNC machine with a head in a barber's chair. Click each row to reveal.

Machined part
Human head + hair
Every row breaks an assumption that industrial automation depends on

Factory robots work so well because everything is predictable: the part is clamped, it doesn't move, it's made of one material, and you can measure exactly whether it came out right.

A haircut breaks every one of those rules. The head moves, hair bends and springs back, every person's hair is different, mistakes can hurt someone, and "a good haircut" is partly a matter of taste.

  • Non-stationary frame: the workpiece frame T_head(t) must be estimated online. Breathing, talking and posture shifts are millimetre-to-centimetre disturbances.
  • Deformable, high-DOF state: about 10⁵ strands with bending/twisting elasticity, friction, cohesion and humidity dependence. The state is only partially observable.
  • Irreversible operations: material removal can't be undone, so you plan conservatively with multi-pass roughing and finishing.
  • Safety-critical contact: human–robot contact near ears and eyes calls for force and speed limits and protective stops.
  • Ill-posed objective: the target is an aesthetic, so you need learned or preference-based quality metrics.

Challenges as identified in Khan & Li (JAIA 2026): head motion compensation, hair property variability, safety-critical control.

Why · lineage

Not a new dream, but a new toolbox

From mechanical patents to robot arms to learned policies. Click a milestone.

Mechanism era · open-loopRobot-arm era · closed-loop, task-specificFrameworks + learning era

People have patented "automatic haircut machines" for decades, but the ideas were ahead of the technology. Cameras, robot arms and computers weren't good, safe or cheap enough.

In the last few years that changed. Researchers now use regular robot arms to comb, style and trim, and our recent surveys try to give the field a common map.

Three eras:

  • Mechanism era (patents): fixed apparatus that maps scalp position to cut length, taught by key positions. Essentially open-loop CAM.
  • Robot-arm era: general-purpose manipulators with vision and force sensing for combing, detangling and styling. These are closed-loop but task-specific.
  • Learning era: teleoperation baselines that produce data, plus foundation models (VLM/VLA) for instruction-conditioned control.

The field was fragmented: perception, hair modelling and motion planning were studied in isolation. Our surveys propose unifying frameworks.

Precision automation · CNC lens

A haircut is a machining job (almost)

Every CNC concept has a haircutting counterpart. Click a row to see where the analogy holds, and where it breaks.

CNC machining
Robotic haircutting

Select a row

The analogy gives us decades of mature theory for free. Its failures show exactly where the research is.

A CNC machine is a computer-controlled cutter in a factory. You give it a 3D design, software turns that into a path for the tool, and the machine follows the path very precisely.

A haircut can be described the same way: the design is the hairstyle, the path is where the clipper goes, and the machine is the robot arm. The big difference is that the "part" is a living person who moves, so the robot has to keep re-measuring.

The CAD → CAM → CNC → metrology chain maps to:

style spec (target length field L*(u,v) on the scalp) → toolpath generation (coverage + multi-pass) → execution (5-DOF: 3 translation + 2 rotation) → evaluation (length/shape error, aesthetics)

Where it breaks: (1) fixturing is replaced by online head-pose estimation and compensation; (2) the material model is a deformable strand field, not a rigid solid, so the cut length depends on the combing/lifting state; (3) tolerances are asymmetric because cutting too short is irreversible.

Khan & Li, JAIA 2026 (CNC-inspired framework). S. Li, Automation 2025, models the head as a workpiece, plans toolpaths as in CNC/CAM, and recommends about 5 DOF.

Precision automation · hardware

Three ways to hold a clipper

Gantry, manipulator or hybrid: each trades precision against reach and safety.

GantryManipulatorHybrid

Gantry

Qualitative comparison for illustration.

Gantry: like a 3D printer frame over the head. Very precise and predictable, but bulky and boxed-in.

Robot arm: like a human arm. It can reach around the head at any angle, but it's harder to make precise and safe.

Hybrid: a frame or rail for big moves plus a small wrist for fine angles, combining the strengths of both.

  • Gantry (Cartesian): decoupled axes, high stiffness, simple kinematics and calibration, bounded workspace. A rotary stage is needed for wrap-around access. Natural CNC fit.
  • Serial manipulator (6–7 DOF): dexterous orientation control, redundancy for avoiding the face and shoulders. Mature cobot safety features (force/torque limits), but posture-dependent stiffness.
  • Hybrid: coarse positioning (rail, gantry or rotary ring) plus a compact wrist. It splits the bandwidth: low-frequency macro motion and high-frequency micro compensation.

Li (2025) argues about 5 DOF (3T + 2R) suffice for clipper work and recommends direct-drive actuation for responsiveness, low backlash and stiffness.

Khan & Li, JAIA 2026 (architectures: gantry-based, manipulator-based, hybrid). S. Li, Automation 2025 (DOF and direct-drive).

Precision automation · perception

What does the robot need to feel?

No single sensor is enough. Toggle the modalities to build up the robot's picture of the head.

VisionForceProximityTactile
Coverage of the robot's needs:

A barber uses eyes, fingertips and a sense of where the scissors are. A robot needs the same, just with different hardware:

  • Cameras see the head, the hair and where the head is moving.
  • Force sensors feel how hard the clipper presses on the scalp.
  • Proximity sensors notice if something (an ear, a hand) is too close.
  • Touch sensors in a comb can feel hair thickness and tangles.
  • Vision (RGB/RGB-D, multi-view): head pose (landmarks plus PnP or learned), hair segmentation, orientation fields, strand/volume reconstruction. Weak under occlusion by the tool and for dark, glossy hair on depth sensors.
  • Force/torque: contact regulation (impedance/admittance), collision detection, comb-drag as a proxy for tangles.
  • Proximity (capacitive, ToF): pre-contact sensing for speed-and-separation monitoring near ears and eyes.
  • Tactile: local strand density and length during lift-and-cut, much like a barber's fingers.

Fusion is typically filter-based: an EKF/UKF on head pose with IMU or visual priors, and force feedback inside the control loop.

Sensing modalities per Khan & Li, JAIA 2026 and IEEE JSAS 2026. Head motion compensation via online pose estimation, visual servoing and inertial sensing (Li, Automation 2025).

Model & plan · hair modelling

Play with the material

Strands are particle chains with stiffness and curl. Comb with the mouse, or pick scissors and drag to cut.

Same scissors, different hair, different result. Cutting coily hair at the same position leaves a very different visible length. The robot has to model this to hit a target style.

To plan a haircut, the robot needs a digital version of your hair. Each strand behaves like a tiny chain that bends, sags under gravity and springs back.

Try the Coily preset and cut: the strand shrinks back into its curls, so the hair ends up much shorter than where you cut. Barbers know this from experience. A robot has to learn or model it.

The demo uses position-based dynamics: Verlet integration, inextensible segment constraints, and a shape-matching term pulling each particle toward a curled rest pose, scaled by stiffness k:

xᵢ ← xᵢ + (xᵢ − xᵢᵖʳᵉᵛ)·d + g·Δt² |xᵢ − xᵢ₋₁| = ℓ (projected) xᵢ ← xᵢ + k·(R(θᵢ)·xᵢʳᵉˢᵗ − xᵢ) (shape)

Modelling spectrum in the literature:

  • Physics-based: mass-spring, Cosserat rods / discrete elastic rods, guide-strand interpolation.
  • Data-driven / neural: single- and multi-view strand reconstruction, implicit volumetric fields, learned orientation maps.

For cutting, the key quantity is the visible length after relaxation L_vis(L_cut, curl, stiffness), which is nonlinear in curl.

Think of mowing a lawn that has a pattern: shorter at the edges, longer in the middle. You need to go everywhere but never cut too short.

Like a careful barber, the robot first takes off most of the length (roughing), then goes back for precise finishing. Doing it in one pass is faster but riskier. Turn it off and watch the red "over-cut" number when the head moves (noise).

Coverage path planning (CPP) on a scalp parameterisation (u,v) with a target length field L*(u,v):

min path length + λ·Σ |L(u,v) − L*(u,v)| s.t. coverage = 100%, L ≥ L* − ε (no over-cut), tool orientation ⟂ scalp, keep-out zones

Boustrophedon/raster, spiral and contour-parallel patterns are classical CPP primitives from mobile robotics and CAM. Multi-pass (roughing → finishing) borrows from CNC: the first pass cuts to L* + δ, the second to L*. In the demo, cut error grows with the material removed per pass (σ_cut ∝ min(1, removal/22 mm), a proxy for clump pulling, deflection and pose noise). A single heavy pass straight to L* therefore over-cuts, while roughing absorbs the error in the δ margin and the light finishing pass stays accurate.

Khan & Li, JAIA 2026 (CNC-adapted coverage planning, multi-pass optimisation). Huang, Khan & Li, IJSS 2026 (trimming as multi-pass coverage under safety and aesthetic constraints).

At 50 mm/s and 100 ms you are already 5 mm off. Prediction buys time, but not during a flinch, so the safety layer must take over.

Even when you try to sit still, your head moves: breathing, talking, looking at your phone. A clipper that ignores this will cut in the wrong place.

Grey ignores the motion. Blue watches the head but always sees it slightly late. Orange also guesses where the head is going next, like catching a ball, and stays closest.

This is the localisation problem of the mobile-robotics lens: estimate T_tool←head(t) online. With sensing delay τ, a pure feedback controller tracks the stale pose x(t−τ), giving error ≈ ẋ·τ. A constant-velocity predictor compensates to first order:

x̂(t) = x(t−τ) + v̂(t−τ)·τ (Smith-style prediction) e_fb ≈ |ẋ|·τ , e_pred ≈ ½|ẍ|·τ²

Prediction fails on abrupt events (flinches), so in practice you combine an EKF over pose with IMU/visual fusion, a bandwidth-limited tool controller, and a safety layer that retracts when the innovation exceeds a threshold.

Layers of safety

Pre-task network and self-checks · sensor redundancy · speed & separation · force limits · protective stop · client-approved paths · clippers over scissors

A haircut happens centimetres from ears, eyes and skin, so safety is not optional. The robot follows a simple rule: the closer it is to something sensitive, the slower it moves, and if something gets too close it stops completely.

Safety comes in layers, like a building with smoke alarms, sprinklers and fire doors. Checks before starting, backup sensors, limits during cutting, and an emergency stop. Using clippers rather than open scissors is itself a safety choice.

Speed-and-separation monitoring with a linear scaling law:

v = v_max · clip( (d − d_stop) / (d_slow − d_stop), 0, 1 ) d < d_stop ⇒ protective stop (latched, manual resume)

Combined with impedance control at contact, F = K·Δx + D·Δẋ with saturated F_max. Additional layers from the literature: network-quality gating before teleoperated tasks, start-up self-diagnostics, redundant sensing, predictive maintenance, soft coverings, proximity-triggered automatic shutdown, and client-approved paths. Certification needs a hazard analysis that ties each layer to a failure mode.

Safety requirements per S. Li, Automation 2025 (network checks, self-diagnostics, redundancy, obstacle avoidance, soft coverings, clippers over scissors, cybersecurity, client-approved paths). Khan & Li, JAIA 2026 (safety-critical control).

TeleHairing (2026): UR10e + clipper, phone camera + AprilTag as the master. Measured loop: 75.6 / 95.7 / 190.5 ms; command RMSE 6.7 / 5.1 / 45.1 mm (9.6 mm without the final withdrawal).

Before robots can cut hair on their own, a human expert can drive the robot remotely, the way a surgeon uses a surgical robot. That's useful today, and every remote haircut also teaches us (and later the AI) how experts move.

The catch is delay. If the barber is far away, the robot reacts late and the barber sees the result late. Try the "Bad link" setting.

TeleHairing (Huang et al., S. Li group) is an operator-in-the-loop baseline: a smartphone runs browser-based monocular AprilTag pose estimation (30 fps) and sends Cartesian commands at 30 Hz to a UR10e, limited to 0.05 m/s and 15°/s, with a separate video channel.

  • Total loop: 75.6 ms local, 95.7 ms cloud relay, 190.5 ms Hong Kong → Finland. Most of the increase is in the master → cloud hop.
  • Command-following RMSE: 6.7 / 5.1 / 45.1 mm. Remote drops to 9.6 mm when the terminal withdrawal is excluded.
  • Detection loss: 23 events in 5 trials, about 2 s gaps, 6.5 mm mean hold drift.

The paper notes its limits: a mannequin study with software timestamps, and an operator confound in remote trials. Classical delay-robust designs are passivity-based teleoperation and Smith predictors (Li, 2025).

Full independence is still far off, so the practical middle step is one human supervising several robots, like one pilot monitoring autopilot, or one worker watching several machines.

The robots do the routine work. When something unusual happens (a client moves, a sensor complains, someone asks a question), the human steps in. The better the robots get, the more of them one person can supervise.

Li (2025) proposes supervised autonomy with an example ratio of 1 barber : 5 robots. Intervention triggers: trajectory deviation, safety-sensor alerts, blocked paths, customer or operator requests.

This is a queueing problem. With per-robot alert rate λ, mean handling time h and N robots, supervisor utilisation is ρ ≈ N·λ·h, and waiting time grows sharply as ρ → 1. Raising autonomy (lower λ) is what allows larger N. The demo lets you feel that trade-off.

Every intervention is also a labelled correction, which makes it valuable training data (next section).

Illustrative concept demo: a rule-based stand-in, not a trained model.

  

    
Today's robots need engineers to program every move. Vision-language-action (VLA) models are a new kind of AI that can look at a scene, read a request in plain language, and directly produce robot movements.
For haircutting, that means you could simply say or show what you want, and the robot would translate it into where to cut and how short. A safety layer still checks every move before it reaches the robot.

    
A VLA is a policy πθ(at:t+H | ot, ℓ, qt) over action chunks, conditioned on images o, language ℓ and proprioception q. It is built on a pretrained vision-language model, with actions either discretised into tokens (RT-2, OpenVLA style) or decoded by a diffusion/flow head (π0 style).
For haircutting we argue for a hierarchical design:
  • High level (VLA/VLM): instruction + images → structured style spec (zone lengths, fade profile) and sub-goals.
  • Mid level: the CNC/coverage planner turns the spec into toolpaths, which keeps verifiability.
  • Low level: a learned residual policy for contact-rich lift-and-cut, inside certified impedance and separation limits.
Why not end-to-end? Data scarcity, irreversibility and certification all favour interpretable intermediate representations.

    
  • Li, Automation 2025: VLA models (e.g., OpenVLA) could connect perception to cutting actions; LLMs for conversation and style recommendation.
  • Representative VLA work: RT-2 (Google DeepMind, 2023), OpenVLA (2024), π0 (Physical Intelligence, 2024).

AI learns from examples, and there are almost no recorded robot haircuts in the world. The plan is to create the examples as we go: barbers driving robots remotely produce expert demonstrations, the AI learns from them, the robot does more on its own, and every time a human corrects it, that correction becomes a new lesson.

VLA · roadmap

  
How autonomous is a haircutting robot?

  
A proposed scale, in the spirit of self-driving cars. Click a level to see who does what.

  

  

    
Bottleneck to the next level

    

      
■ Robot■ Human

  

  

    
Self-driving cars are described in levels from 0 (you drive) to 5 (the car does everything). The same idea helps for haircutting robots.
Today, research is around level 1–2: robots driven by people, or following set paths on a mannequin. The realistic next goal is level 3, where robots handle routine cuts and a human supervises several of them.

    
A proposed taxonomy (ours, by analogy to SAE J3016), defined by who owns perception, planning, execution and fallback:
  • L0 smart tools (guarded clippers) · L1 teleoperation / assisted positioning · L2 scripted autonomous paths, 1:1 supervision, static head · L3 online head compensation + routine styles, 1:N supervision, human is the fallback · L4 instruction- and image-conditioned styles across hair types, remote support only · L5 unrestricted.
The bottlenecks are different at each step: L2→L3 is head-motion robustness and certification; L3→L4 is generalisation across hair types and styles (VLA plus data); L4→L5 is open-world safety and trust.

    
Taxonomy proposed for this talk. Li, Automation 2025: full autonomy "currently not feasible"; commercial use likely within five years via teleoperation and supervised autonomy.

  







  
Open problems

  
What we still don't know

  
Eight open problems, and a good place for mathematical modelling. Click to flip.

  

  

    
These are the questions that still stand between today's prototypes and a robot you'd trust with your own hair. Many are fundamentally modelling questions: how hair behaves, how heads move, how to measure a "good" haircut, which fits this workshop well.

    
Where mathematical models are needed most:
  • Reduced-order hair mechanics that run in real time and predict post-cut visible length.
  • Stochastic head-motion models (spectral + jump processes) for robust MPC and chance-constrained planning.
  • Coverage planning on curved manifolds with irreversible operations, as a constrained optimisation over the scalp.
  • Formal safety: control barrier functions over separation and force; verification of learned policies.
  • Quality metrics: learned aesthetic or preference models plus geometric error on reconstructed hair.

    
Synthesised from Khan & Li (JAIA 2026, IEEE JSAS 2026), Huang, Khan & Li (IJSS 2026), Li (Automation 2025), and arXiv:2411.05137 (inclusion across hair types).

  







  
Outlook

  
What does the future look like?

  
Step through time. Three settings where robotic haircutting changes who gets care, and how.

  
TodayNext ~5 yearsLong term

  

  

    
In the near future you're most likely to meet these robots in salons, airports and malls, supervised by staff, or driven by a barber from another city.
The most meaningful impact may be in care homes and hospitals, for people who can't easily get to a barber. Longer term, a general-purpose assistant might help with several grooming tasks at home.

    
Deployment models discussed in the literature: robotic salons (malls, airports, offices), a remote-barber platform (ride-hailing analogy), and revenue from hardware, maintenance and digital services. Li (2025) calls commercial use "highly likely" within five years, starting with teleoperation and supervised autonomy.
Long-term technical enablers: humanoid/bimanual platforms, VLA foundation policies fine-tuned on grooming data, VR/haptic interfaces for remote experts, and certification frameworks for close-contact service robots.

    
S. Li, Automation 2025 (business models, roadmap, VR/haptics/LLMs). Khan & Li, JAIA 2026 (healthcare and personal-care applications).

  







  
Take-home

  
From precision automation to understanding

  

    

      
1 · Reuse mature theory
CNC and mobile-robotics frameworks give haircutting a rigorous backbone: localisation, coverage, control.

      
2 · Humans in the loop are the bridge
Teleoperation and supervised autonomy are useful today and generate the data learning needs.

      
3 · VLA models: high-level understanding, verified low-level control
Language and vision set the goal; certified planners and controllers keep it safe.

      
4 · It's a modelling problem
Hair mechanics, head motion, quality metrics: open invitations to this community.
